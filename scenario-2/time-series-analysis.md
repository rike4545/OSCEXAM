# Time-Series Addendum — FY 2019 through FY 2026

## Executive conclusion

The post-agreement evidence raises substantial questions about whether the 2018 Health Benefits Agreement's $600 million recurring annual savings commitment was actually delivered in a durable, independently verifiable way. The most important evidence is not simply that City health-insurance costs continued to rise; gross spending can rise even when savings are achieved against a counterfactual baseline. The stronger concern is that the Comptroller's December 2025 audit found that the recurring savings evidence supplied for the predecessor agreement had not been independently reviewed or updated after 2018, that HISF had become insolvent, and that liabilities were materially underreported. The FY 2026 ACFR then disclosed a $937 million write-down of old HISF receivables and $969 million of accrued payments to covered entities at year-end.

**Do not state that the City definitively failed to achieve the 2018 $600 million savings target.** The evidence reviewed establishes a serious verification and fund-solvency problem, but it does not provide a complete independent actuarial calculation of the 2018 agreement's savings performance year by year. The audit's specific statement about unverified recurring savings refers to the $303.5 million recurring savings commitment under the 2014 agreement. The 2018 agreement's $600 million recurring target is distinct and should be tested separately.

## 1. HISF reconstructed financial position

The Comptroller's audit reconstructed the Fund's position by deducting liabilities from reported assets. Amounts below are millions of dollars.

| Period | Reported assets | Unreported liabilities | Reconstructed balance |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| FY 2019 | 1,587.6 | — | 1,587.6 |
| FY 2020 | 1,369.6 | 294.0 | 1,075.6 |
| FY 2021 | 1,031.8 | 949.8 | 82.0 |
| FY 2022 | 900.0 | 1,868.7 | (968.6) |
| FY 2023 | 995.0 | 2,906.3 | (1,911.2) |
| FY 2024* | 842.9 | 2,906.3 | (2,063.3) |
| April 2025* | 1,023.8 | 3,115.1 | (2,091.3) |

*FY 2024 and April 2025 exclude equalization payment amounts for FY 2024 that had not yet been calculated; the liabilities and deficit could therefore be larger. Source: NYC Comptroller audit FK24-070A, Table XV.

This series indicates that the reconstructed balance deteriorated sharply between FY 2021 and FY 2023. It is a Fund solvency series, not a direct measurement of the 2018 agreement's savings. It should not be presented as a savings realization schedule.

## 2. Audit findings relevant to savings verification

The December 30, 2025 Comptroller audit found:

- HISF was insolvent and unable to meet its obligations.
- The Fund had approximately $3.1 billion in liabilities through FY 2023 for equalization payments owed to the City and amounts owed to vendors as of April 2025.
- Approximately $3.3 billion in offsets to equalization payments reduced the Fund's balance between FY 2015 and FY 2023, in lieu of savings the MLC committed to generating under the 2014 and 2018 agreements. The auditors expressly noted that they formed no opinion on the accuracy of the savings estimates.
- The audit stated that the recurring $303.5 million savings promised under the **2014** agreement had supporting documentation generated by healthcare providers in 2018, but the documentation had not subsequently been reviewed or verified by an independent healthcare actuary and had not been updated.
- The audit did not supply an independent year-by-year actuarial validation of the separate $600 million recurring annual commitment in the **2018** agreement.

This distinction is essential. The audit is strong evidence of HISF's insolvency, governance, reporting, and accounting problems. It is not a final actuarial verdict that the 2018 agreement's $600 million savings were zero or never achieved.

## 3. Later initiatives and the changing financial picture

| Initiative | Timing | Projected savings | Status / analytical treatment |
|---|---|---:|---|
| 2018 HBA recurring savings | FY 2021 onward | $600M annually | Agreement commitment; do not treat as independently verified actual savings without actuarial support. |
| Medicare Advantage Plus | Proposed 2021, intended 2022 start | About $600M annually | Not implemented; City later abandoned the initiative. This was intended to help HISF and is not itself proof that the HBA commitment was met. |
| New active/pre-Medicare health plan (NYCE PPO) | Effective Jan. 1, 2026 | Up to $900M–$1B annually in public projections | New initiative replacing GHI-CBP; projected savings, not yet an established full-year realized amount. Do not automatically attribute it to the 2018 HBA without a formal accounting/actuarial reconciliation. |

The June 2025 Comptroller fiscal note estimated at least $612 million of FY 2025 fiscal risk due to exhaustion of HISF disposable balances. It also reported that the Fund's disposable balance at the end of FY 2024 was approximately $1 million. The fiscal note identified the planned Medicare Advantage switch and new active/pre-Medicare plan procurement as the two major initiatives connected to the 2018 HBA's savings agenda.

The FY 2026 ACFR adds important subsequent evidence:
- City health insurance costs were $1.542 billion above the FY 2026 Adopted Budget, a 15.7% variance.
- $937 million of that variance was caused by writing down HISF receivables accrued in FY 2020–FY 2022.
- Excluding the write-down, health insurance costs were still $605 million (6.2%) above the Adopted Budget.
- The City had $969 million in outstanding accrued payments to covered entities at FY 2026 year-end for health-premium differentials and their proportionate share of collectively bargained healthcare savings.
- The NYCE PPO plan was effective January 1, 2026; the ACFR does not establish a full-year realized savings result for the new plan.

The $937 million write-down is a particularly important bridge between the agreement's accounting structure and later fiscal exposure: receivables that had been accrued on the assumption that HISF would reimburse the City were written off when the Fund could not support them. This is not a direct measure of the $600 million annual savings target, but it demonstrates that prior expected recoveries did not translate into collectible resources.

## 4. Separate gross expenditure trend from savings against baseline

A rising health-insurance expenditure trend does not, by itself, prove that an agreed savings target was missed. The correct test is:

**Verified savings = comparable baseline cost without the initiative − actual comparable cost with the initiative**, adjusted for membership, plan design, premium rates, utilization, demographics, benefit changes, and other relevant factors.

The analysis should keep three measures separate:
1. **Gross City health-insurance expenditure** — the City's total recorded cost.
2. **Savings credited under the agreement** — including any authorized offsets to equalization payments or other contractual accounting credits.
3. **Realized, recurring healthcare cost reductions** — actuarially validated savings that persist in future years.

The agreement's equalization-offset mechanism could reduce the City's transfer obligation to HISF without necessarily producing a corresponding reduction in medical claims or gross healthcare costs. This is why a reconciliation of offsets, plan costs, cash transfers, and liabilities is necessary.

## 5. Suggested briefing conclusion

> The post-FY 2021 record raises material concerns about the durability and verification of the 2018 health-benefit savings arrangement. The City continued to carry the $600 million annual savings assumption in later financial plans, but the Stabilization Fund deteriorated into insolvency, with approximately $3.1 billion in liabilities identified by the Comptroller through FY 2023 and April 2025. The FY 2026 ACFR subsequently recorded a $937 million write-down of prior HISF receivables and $969 million in accrued payments to covered entities. These figures do not, on their own, prove that the 2018 agreement's $600 million recurring savings target was not achieved; they do show that the accounting credits and fund resources expected to support the arrangement were not financially sustainable. A definitive compliance assessment requires an independent actuarial reconciliation of the agreement's baseline, annual savings claims, offsets, and recurring cost reductions. The new NYCE PPO plan may provide significant future savings, but its projected savings should be reported separately until realized and formally reconciled to the prior agreement.

## 6. OLR's official FY2019–FY2021 savings report: what the City counted

The Office of Labor Relations' December 27, 2021 *Final Report on FY 19–FY 21 Health Care Savings* is the City's own published accounting of the initial agreement period. OLR states that the City and MLC met the $1.1 billion three-year target. Its attached schedule reports $1.217 billion in gross savings, less $59 million above the FY2021 $600 million cap, for $1.158 billion credited toward the target.

| Reported component | FY2019–FY2021 |
|---|---:|
| Rollover savings from FY2015–FY2018 agreement | $205M |
| HIP HMO rate savings | $701M |
| Senior Care plan rate savings, net | $19M |
| WIN Fertility | $28M |
| Site-of-service / infusion / surgical site-of-care | $15M |
| Empire length-of-stay management | $134M |
| Centers of Excellence in CBP | -$1.2M |
| Emblem drug changes | $117M |
| **Gross total reported by OLR** | **$1,217M** |
| Less amount above FY2021 $600M cap | **-$59M** |
| **Adjusted amount credited toward FY2019–FY2021 target** | **$1,158M** |

OLR's table reports $193 million in FY2019, $365 million in FY2020, and $600 million in FY2021 after the cap. OLR also explains that the HIP HMO rate savings were calculated from the difference between budgeted and finalized premium-rate increases, with the differential described as an amount that otherwise would have been paid by the City into the Stabilization Fund. This is important: the $701 million HIP rate component is not presented as a direct reduction in paid medical claims. It is a counterfactual premium-rate/equalization calculation.

The report separately says the proposed retiree Medicare Advantage program was expected to save about $600 million annually for HISF, and explicitly says those savings were earmarked for the Fund and did not count toward the $1.1 billion agreement target.

### What the OLR report establishes—and what it does not

- It establishes that OLR publicly **reported** that the City and MLC met the $1.1 billion cumulative FY2019–FY2021 target.
- It provides a component-level schedule and identifies the calculation logic for the major premium-rate component.
- It does **not**, by itself, independently validate the actuarial assumptions, quantify all counterfactual risk, or demonstrate that $600 million in savings persisted on a recurring basis in every year after FY2021.
- The 2018 agreement required the $600 million annual amount after FY2021 to be recurring and agreed within FY2021. The OLR report's adjusted FY2021 total of $600 million should not automatically be treated as proof that the recurring post-FY2021 condition was satisfied.
- The report notes that CBP rates exceeded HIP rates in FY2021 and FY2022, accelerating the expected decline in HISF, and that future CBP-derived savings depended on offsets to HISF assets.

This makes the OLR report a key primary source for the City's compliance claim, but the separate recurring-savings question still requires post-FY2021 actuarial support and reconciliation to actual plan costs, equalization payments, offsets, and Fund liabilities.
